ENG TEACHER — A FRIENDLY AI ENGLISH SPEAKING COMPANION
Detailed product idea and realistic development direction
Prepared: 17 November 2026 (Asia/Calcutta)
Status: Product proposal. Planned features are not claims of completed work.

1. THE IDEA IN ONE SENTENCE

A learner opens a friendly, video-call-style English practice session with an
AI teacher, speaks naturally in English or a supported home language, and gets
patient conversation, useful English alternatives, and a chance to try again.

The teacher can eventually appear as an original female or male animated
character, with a chosen licensed voice. It should feel approachable and calm.
It must clearly identify itself as AI; it must not pretend to be a real person.
An audio-only experience remains available when the learner prefers it, the
connection is weak, or the avatar fails. The learner's camera is optional and
is not needed for the first product.

The central promise is: "Say what you mean. We will help you express it in
English, without making you feel embarrassed."

2. THE PROBLEM AND THE PEOPLE WE WANT TO HELP

Many learners know vocabulary and grammar exercises but hesitate when speaking.
They translate in their heads, fear mistakes, lose confidence when interrupted,
or cannot find a patient practice partner. Some understand English but find it
hard to answer an interview question or explain a simple problem at work.

Start with consenting adult Hindi-English learners because this repository
already has that foundation. Bengali/Bangla-English is a priority expansion,
with separate review by fluent speakers. Later language choices should follow
demonstrated learner demand, speech-provider coverage, and local evaluation.

Useful early situations include job interviews, introducing yourself, customer
calls, workplace updates, shopping, asking directions, and everyday friendship.
The product should help someone communicate clearly, rather than pressure them
to erase their accent or imitate a single national identity.

3. WHAT A GOOD SESSION SHOULD FEEL LIKE

The learner chooses a goal, explanation language, correction preference, and
short session length. A beginner might select "Everyday English, Bangla help,
gentle corrections." An intermediate learner might select "Job interview,
English explanations, feedback after I finish."

The teacher opens with a simple question, listens, and allows pauses. It replies
to the meaning of the answer before offering one important improvement. It
might say: "I understand. A natural way to say that is ... Would you like to
try it once?" It should not sound like an examiner correcting every word.

If the learner asks for a translation, slower speech, another example, or a
repeat, the teacher stays with the same practice target. It advances only
after an appropriate retry, a completed answer, or an explicit skip. If the
learner interrupts, the teacher stops speaking and listens.

The learner can mute, end, skip, ask for help, or switch to audio-only at any
time. Captions, readable correction cards, keyboard controls, and clear status
indicators make the experience usable without relying on facial animation.

At the end, the learner sees a small recap: a few useful sentences, what was
practiced, and one next step. Saving these items should be optional. A lesson
should end with a concrete success, not an invented fluency percentage.

4. EXAMPLES OF THE TEACHER'S BEHAVIOR

A. Gentle correction of an English sentence

Learner: "Yesterday I go market."
Teacher: "You can say, 'Yesterday, I went to the market.' Because it happened
yesterday, use 'went.' Would you like to try that?"
Learner: "Please explain in Bangla."
Teacher, when Bangla support has been validated: "গতকালের ঘটনা বোঝাতে 'go'-এর
পরিবর্তে 'went' ব্যবহার করি।" It then repeats the same target sentence and waits.

This exchange is a proposed behavior example, not evidence of tested Bangla
speech quality. A bilingual teacher must review the explanation and voice.

B. Helping a learner express a Bangla thought

Learner: "আমি আজ অফিসে যেতে পারব না।"
Intended English: "I won't be able to go to the office today."
Teacher: "You could say, 'I won't be able to go to the office today.' In a
message to your manager, 'I won't be able to come to work today' may fit too."

The teacher should explain that "go" versus "come" depends on the speaker's
and listener's perspective. It must not add an illness, family emergency, or
other reason that the learner did not give.

C. Preserving negation, names, and numbers

Learner: "আমি টাকা দিইনি।"
Teacher: "You can say, 'I didn't pay the money.'"
It must never silently turn this into "I paid the money."

Learner: "My name is Aditi. I worked there for twelve months."
The teacher must preserve Aditi and twelve. If recognition is uncertain, it
asks whether it heard the name or number correctly before building feedback.

D. Accepting correct English

Learner: "I handled customer calls at my previous job."
Teacher: "That is clear. What kind of questions did customers usually ask?"
An optional polished alternative can be labeled as a style choice, not an error.

E. Offering alternatives without changing the learner's identity

Learner: "I want improve my English for office."
Teacher: "You can say, 'I want to improve my English for work.' Another option
is, 'I want to communicate more confidently at work.' The second option adds
an emphasis on confidence; choose it only if that is what you mean."

F. Recognizing uncertainty

If audio is unclear, the teacher says: "Did you say you can attend or cannot
attend?" Asking once is better than confidently teaching the opposite meaning.

5. THE TEACHING RULES

Keep four things distinct: the recognized original words, the proposed English
sentence, the teacher's explanation, and any audio-based pronunciation result.
The recognized transcript is an estimate of what was said, not perfect truth.

Correct at most one important issue per turn by default. Prioritize errors that
affect meaning or the learner's current goal. Let the learner finish. Accept
natural equivalent retries, while checking that names, quantities, tense, and
negation still express the intended message. Do not demand verbatim repetition
when another correct sentence works.

Provide gentle, balanced, and intensive modes later. Even in intensive mode,
corrections should be useful, respectful, and controllable. Never manufacture
mistakes, shame an accent, or confuse informal English with incorrect English.

The teacher should ask a relevant follow-up question so the interaction remains
a conversation. Repeating a sentence once is practice; using the same pattern
on a different question tomorrow is stronger evidence of learning.

6. LANGUAGE SUPPORT: A ROADMAP, NOT AN "ALL LANGUAGES" CLAIM

There are separate capabilities for each language: recognizing speech,
understanding mixed-language sentences, translating meaning into English,
explaining grammar, and speaking a clear explanation aloud. A provider listing
a language does not prove that every one of these tasks works well.

The product should publish an honest support matrix: experimentally available,
human-reviewed pilot, supported for a defined workflow, or unavailable. Test
Bengali/Bangla with varied speakers, regional vocabulary, English words inside
Bangla sentences, names, slow speech, and ordinary phone microphones.

Start with the existing Hindi-English slice, then validate Bengali-English.
Expand to other languages only after provider trials and native-speaker review.
Low-resource languages and dialects may need different models or paid reviewers.
When support is weak, say so and offer English-only or typed help instead of
inventing a translation. Universal perfect language support is not a credible
launch promise.

7. THE ON-SCREEN TEACHER

The initial visual experience can be an original, clearly animated teacher
with listening, thinking, speaking, and idle states. Offer female and male
character options without suggesting one teaches better. Additional appearances
and voices can follow if users find them helpful and the assets are licensed.

Lip movement should follow the teacher's actual audio. One component must own
audio playback; the avatar and LiveKit renderer must not both play the same
voice. Interruption, mute, reconnect, cleanup, and audio-only fallback must work
before visual realism becomes a priority.

A highly realistic generated human video is a possible research direction, but
it adds inference cost, latency, asset rights, device load, and uncanny results.
Do not copy a real person's likeness or clone a voice without the required
permission and rights. A simple reliable character is a useful product already.

8. WHAT ALREADY EXISTS IN THIS PROJECT

This is not a blank repository. The active foundation contains a responsive
Next.js/React web client, a Python LiveKit voice worker, configurable OpenAI
recognition/model/speech adapters, CPU voice-activity detection, interview
questions, correction/retry logic, captions, separate original/suggested cards,
session controls, a ten-minute deadline, and a temporary in-page recap.

Server-side session code issues restricted room tokens and uses a pilot access
code. It is an invited-pilot access mechanism, not finished individual account
authentication or production billing. The app does not intentionally persist
lesson recordings. External providers still process audio/text under their own
terms, which need separate review.

Earlier cloud onboarding verified dependency installation, build/type checks,
14 JavaScript tests, 42 Python tests, six synthetic Chromium browser checks,
Python linting, the agent CLI, and CPU voice-activity inference. Those were
local/offline checks in that environment; they are not proof of a real paid
speech lesson, Bengali support, Android hardware performance, or avatar quality.
Current changes require their own appropriate validation.

The Cadence and Talking Avatar archives are preserved as reference material.
Their presence does not mean their features are integrated. Existing research,
the 21-repository inventory, prior decisions, and continuity notes must remain
available. Historical PR descriptions must not be treated as merged code without
checking current Git state. Build on verified work rather than replacing it.

9. PRACTICAL TECHNICAL APPROACH

Keep one monorepo with clear boundaries. TypeScript is suitable for the website,
browser audio controls, events, and avatar. Python is suitable for the existing
voice agent, speech adapters, evaluation, and later audio/ML experiments. One
product does not require one programming language. Rewriting everything into
one language now would consume effort without proving better teaching.

The main path is: microphone -> LiveKit transport -> speech recognition ->
meaning and tutor policy -> validated teaching response -> speech generation ->
teacher audio, captions, and optional avatar. Preserve replaceable provider
interfaces so a later model comparison does not require a new application.

Use versioned event schemas between browser and worker. Treat browser events,
provider responses, and stored lesson data as untrusted input. Validate structured
decisions and preserve session state when a provider fails. Measure recognition,
model, synthesis, network, and rendering delays separately before optimizing.

Add one original lesson definition and one tested feature at a time. Reuse
licensed SDKs selectively. Do not merge an unrelated full application, duplicate
authentication systems, or introduce several competing audio pipelines merely
because a reference repository includes them.

10. PRONUNCIATION, PROGRESS, AND PERSONALIZATION

Initially the teacher can model a sentence, slow it down, and let the learner
repeat it. A transcript match establishes neither accurate pronunciation nor a
native accent. Reliable pronunciation coaching needs the actual audio, a defined
target, a suitable model, and calibration against human judgments across accents,
noise levels, and devices. If uncertain, offer practice rather than a score.

Later, learners may opt to save useful phrases, goals, recurring mistakes, and
completed practice. A small placement activity can propose an approximate level;
it should not present an official examination result. Spaced review can bring
back a phrase in a new situation instead of repeating the same prompt forever.

Memory must be visible and editable. The teacher should say "You chose to save
this phrase" rather than imply hidden surveillance. Personalization should use
the minimum necessary information. Friend-like conversation means warmth and
patience, not pressure to depend on the AI or disclose private experiences.

11. PRIVACY, TRUST, AND OPERATIONS

Recording stays off by default. Saving practice history, retaining audio for
evaluation, and using data for training require distinct choices. Provide clear
retention, export, and deletion behavior before offering stored personal history.
Do not log learner utterances or credentials as routine diagnostics. Review
provider processing, retention, region, and contractual terms explicitly.

Before opening a paid public service, add individual authentication, authorization,
distributed quotas, spending caps, abuse controls, sanitized monitoring, backups,
and recovery procedures. Provider keys stay server-side. Dependency and asset
licenses need review for the exact distribution, not only the upstream README.

Start with adult volunteers. A child-focused product requires a separate privacy,
consent, age-appropriate teaching, and safeguarding design. The language teacher
is not a substitute for professional medical, legal, or mental-health advice.

Repository structure recovery cannot establish that an account is secure. If
account compromise is suspected, the owner must separately review GitHub access,
sessions, integrations, keys, and recovery settings. Preserve history and classify
unexpected files from evidence; do not label an author malicious without proof.

12. A REALISTIC DELIVERY PATH

The periods below are planning horizons, not delivery commitments. Each phase
depends on evidence, funding, maintainers, reviewers, and the previous gates.

First 0-3 months: prove the existing voice foundation.
- Complete real provider setup and desktop/physical Android voice validation.
- Exercise Hindi-English speech, interruptions, help/retry, reconnect, and the
  complete ten-minute deadline. Fix observed failures rather than guessed ones.
- Measure all provider usage and retry costs, latency, and failure rates.
- Trial one Bengali-English workflow with bilingual review; label it experimental.
- Add one original everyday or interview exercise and a small teacher-reviewed set.
- Trial one optional avatar only after audio is reliable; preserve fallback.

Months 3-12: turn the proven slice into a useful controlled product.
- Improve robust help/retry intent handling and meaning-preserving alternatives.
- Build reviewed lesson content, learner-controlled progress, accounts, and deletion.
- Expand Bengali support only when its defined acceptance checks pass.
- Run an adult learner pilot and evaluate next-day transfer and willingness to pay.
- Trial audio-based pronunciation feedback and keep uncertain outcomes unscored.
- Harden operations and assess whether subscriptions cover real service costs.

Years 1-2: expand from evidence.
- Add languages selected by demand and native-speaker evaluation, not a checkbox.
- Develop adaptive courses, educator tools, accessible practice, and better review.
- Compare avatar approaches and ordinary Android hardware performance.
- Consider a native mobile client when browser limitations justify its maintenance.
- Benchmark appropriately licensed open models against hosted models using the
  same held-out tasks, total operating cost, and quality measures.

Beyond two years: research options, not promises.
- Explore efficient on-device/offline practice for selected devices and languages.
- Consider permissioned model adaptation, richer pronunciation feedback, and
  controlled group roleplays if evaluation shows a learning benefit.
- Reassess realistic interactive video teachers as models and hardware improve.
- Offer educator partnerships and additional language pairs only with quality,
  rights, support, and economics that the team can maintain.

The people responsible are the owner/product lead, application and voice
engineers, bilingual teachers/reviewers, a designer, test learners, and later
security/operations and ML specialists where needed. AI coding tools can help
implement and test work; they cannot replace account ownership, consent,
native-speaker judgment, device testing, or evidence of learning outcomes.

13. WHAT CANNOT BE PROMISED, AND WHAT TO DO INSTEAD

Perfect understanding of every language, dialect, accent, and noisy utterance:
not achievable as an unconditional promise. Publish supported conditions, test
them, ask clarifying questions, and improve known weak cases.

Knowing the learner's true intention when speech is ambiguous: not possible
without more information. Ask a short question rather than invent a fact.

Exact pronunciation scoring from text alone: the needed acoustic information
is absent. Use consented audio and a validated assessor, or provide no score.

Zero latency, zero failures, and unlimited high-quality AI at zero operating
cost: not realistic. Measure, optimize, limit usage, and communicate failures.

Guaranteed fluency, a job offer, an exam result, or a native accent within a
fixed number of days: not supportable. Offer a practice plan and measure specific
skills over time. Results depend on the learner and many external factors.

A photorealistic teacher on every cheap phone with no network or processing
tradeoffs: not established. Offer lightweight avatars/audio-only first, and
evaluate heavier rendering separately. Full offline multilingual voice remains
device-, model-, and language-dependent even if future technology improves.

A real human friend who understands and feels everything: the system is AI.
Make it friendly while explaining its nature and keeping the learner in control.

Owning all underlying AI technology just because the app code is ours: not true.
SDKs, models, voices, assets, and hosted services retain their licenses and terms.
Increase independence by maintaining adapters and evaluating licensed alternatives.

Future AI progress may make some expensive features cheaper or more accurate.
It does not remove ambiguity, data rights, consent, or the need for validation.
Review research options after one year, two years, and later without assuming
that the calendar alone will solve them.

14. HOW WE WILL KNOW THE PRODUCT IS HELPING

Begin with the existing proposed evaluation: 30 teacher-reviewed mixed-language
turns and 20 already-acceptable English turns, with separate sets per language
when expanding. Earlier provisional targets were at least 27/30 meaning-preserved
mixed turns and no more than 1/20 false corrections. These are small initial
gates, not statistical proof of broad quality and not achieved results.

Measure p50 and p90 end-of-speech-to-first-audible-response latency, interruption
success, session completion, recovery, and device failures. The earlier median
under-two-second goal is aspirational until real measurements support it.

For learning, check whether a learner can reuse a practiced pattern on a new
question the next day, with consent and a fair comparison. Track confidence,
voluntary return, teacher-rated correctness, and useful practice completed.
Do not substitute time in app, an avatar screenshot, or an artificial score for
evidence of improvement.

Calculate total cost per completed lesson: recognition minutes, model input/output,
speech generation, LiveKit/media, hosting, retries, support, and taxes where
applicable. Old illustrative provider prices are not current quotations. Begin
with a small adult pilot; choose prices and spending limits after observing
real cost and willingness to pay. Avoid selling unlimited usage before testing
its economics.

15. THE NEXT PRACTICAL PRODUCT STEP

Preserve the working foundation, reference material, and project history. Keep
unexpected or deferred material in the documented AnalyseFuture area rather
than silently deleting it. Complete the repository organization and save this
idea first, as requested by the owner.

In a subsequent owner-directed implementation task, choose one measurable next
increment: complete privacy-safe usage/error metrics or validate one real
Hindi-English voice lesson. Then address observed conversation failures and
run a small Bengali-English experiment. Add visual polish after the speech
and teaching loop work reliably.

The long-term product is a patient speaking companion that helps learners move
from "I know it in my language" to "I can say it clearly in English." Its success
will come from reliable conversation, respectful teaching, and demonstrated
learning—not from claiming every possible AI feature on the first day.
